# Chapter 1 — Epistemic Priority and Ontological Commitment

## Before the Theory

Before there is a theory, there is an occurrence.

Something appears.

A color. A pressure. A thought. The recognition of a word. The feeling that a
sentence is obvious, irritating, or not yet clear. The content changes, but the
minimum fact remains: experience occurs.

This is a smaller claim than it may seem.

It does not yet establish a permanent self. It does not establish that the
world is exactly as it appears. It does not tell us whether experience is
produced by a brain, realized by a brain, filtered through a brain, distributed
through a larger physical system, or fundamental to reality. It does not prove
that consciousness existed before organisms or that it continues after the
organism dies.

It says only what must be said before any of those possibilities can be
examined.

> **Observation — DOT-E0**
>
> Experience occurs.

The term *experience* will initially carry as little machinery as possible. It
means that there is something present rather than nothing present from the
first-person side. Thomas Nagel described the relevant distinction through the
question of what it is like to be an organism. Ned Block later distinguished
phenomenal consciousness—experience itself—from access-consciousness, the
availability of information for reasoning, report, and control of action
(Nagel 1974; Block 1995). The distinction is not the final word, but it blocks a
confusion that would otherwise follow us through the whole book.

Experience is not identical to the ability to describe experience.

It is not identical to wakefulness.

It is not identical to attention, intelligence, memory, self-reflection, or
behavioral responsiveness.

These capacities interact. They must not be collapsed.

Even DOT-E0 carries a burden. Illusionist accounts argue that the mind
misrepresents itself as possessing special phenomenal properties and that the
scientific problem is to explain those representations and dispositions rather
than to posit irreducible qualia (Frankish 2016). DOT cannot answer this view by
saying that illusionism is obviously absurd. An illusionist does not need to
deny that brains discriminate, represent, judge, report, and insist that
experience seems present. The disagreement concerns what, if anything, those
processes reveal about phenomenal properties.

The complete theory must therefore avoid loading DOT-E0 with everything it
eventually wants to prove. The datum is not that experience is immaterial,
cosmic, unified, continuous, or intrinsically meaningful. Those are additional
claims.

The datum is occurrence.

## Occurrence and Content

The first discipline is to distinguish the occurrence of experience from the
accuracy of its content.

A straight stick can look bent in water. A harmless sound can be experienced
as danger. A remembered conversation can feel exact and still be wrong. A
person can be certain about another person's intention and have misread it
completely.

In each case, error does not imply that nothing occurred. It identifies a
difference between what was experienced and what the experience was taken to
mean.

This gives us a second starting observation:

> **Observation — DOT-E1**
>
> The content of experience can be inaccurate even while an occurrence of
> experience is present.

This is why subjective data must not be treated as either noise or revelation.

If I feel fear, the feeling is evidence that a state of fear is occurring. It
is not, by itself, proof that the object of fear is dangerous. If I feel
certainty, certainty is part of the event to be explained. It is not a warranty
on the proposition I believe. If pain is reported, the report matters. The
location, cause, memory, interpretation, and language of that pain can still be
investigated.

Feeling is data.

Feeling is not automatically truth.

The public edition introduced this as the Subjective Data Principle. The
complete theory must now make the principle precise enough to survive use. It
does not grant each person infallible access to the mechanisms producing their
experience. It grants each person a form of access that no external observer
possesses in the same way: the occurrence is present for the one undergoing
it.

That access can be immediate while its interpretation is learned, partial, and
revisable.

## What Epistemic Priority Means

Before an instrument can support a conclusion for you, something from the
instrument must become available to you.

Before a paper can change your mind, you must encounter some representation of
its argument.

Before a measurement becomes evidence in your reasoning, the number, image,
sound, memory, or proposition must enter a form you can experience and use.

Digital Organism Theory calls this **epistemic priority**.

> **Definition — DOT-D2**
>
> Epistemic priority means that evidence usable by a subject becomes available
> to that subject through conscious experience.

The definition concerns access to evidence, not the origin of the evidence.

Let (e) be an event or trace that can function as evidence, (r(e)) a
representation through which a subject can access it, and (S) the subject.
The modest relation is:

\[
\operatorname{UsesAsEvidence}(S,e)
\;\Rightarrow\;
\operatorname{Experiences}(S,r(e))
\]

This is a constraint on what it means for evidence to become usable by a
conscious inquirer. It is not a law claiming that the original event is made of
experience. A star can emit light before anyone observes it. A detector can
register a collision while every researcher sleeps. A computer can transform
data through processes no person consciously follows. Unconscious activity can
shape judgment before a conclusion reaches awareness.

Epistemic priority does not deny any of this.

It says that when a trace becomes a reason *for a subject*—when it participates
in that subject's conscious comparison, doubt, interpretation, or decision—it
does so through some experienced presentation. The detector output and the
experience of reading it are not the same event. The first can be independent
of the second. The second is still required for the output to become knowledge
the inquirer can consciously evaluate.

The observer belongs in the account of knowing.

That does not mean the observer manufactures whatever is known.

## Objectivity Does Not Require the Absence of an Observer

Science became powerful by refusing to let one person's conviction settle a
public question.

Measurement externalizes comparison. Instruments extend sensitivity.
Calibration reveals drift. Blinding reduces expectancy effects. Replication
tests whether a result survives changes of observer, laboratory, sample, and
time. Statistical methods estimate how much apparent structure could arise
without the proposed cause. Adversarial methods give competing theories a role
in deciding, before results are known, what different outcomes should mean.

None of these practices removes experience from existence.

They discipline the path from experience to claim.

Objectivity should therefore not be defined as a view from nowhere. In practice,
it is a family of procedures for making observations less dependent on the
preferences, limitations, and private authority of any one observer. A result
becomes public not because no one experiences it, but because many differently
positioned observers can examine a stable trace through declared methods and
arrive at constrained judgments.

First-person methods need the same discipline.

Varela proposed neurophenomenology as a program of mutual constraint between
carefully examined experience and cognitive science (Varela 1996). Lutz and
colleagues later used trained first-person reports to define trial clusters and
related those clusters to distinct patterns of neural synchrony during a visual
task (Lutz et al. 2002). That work did not convert introspection into certainty.
It showed a more useful possibility: experiential distinctions can guide public
analysis when the methods for producing, grouping, and comparing those
distinctions are explicit.

The direction of constraint must run both ways.

A first-person report can reveal a distinction an experimental average has
hidden. Neural and behavioral evidence can reveal that a person's explanation
of their own state is incomplete. Repeated observation can refine the language
of experience. Unexpected public evidence can force revision of the categories
used to report it.

The observer belongs in the method.

The observer does not rule the method.

## Two Conditions of Access

Consciousness research works across an asymmetry that cannot be removed by
confidence.

My experience is present to me in a way your experience is not. Your expression,
speech, decisions, physiology, and neural activity can become evidence for me.
Your experience itself is not presented to me as mine is. I encounter traces of
your experience and infer the most responsible account of what those traces
mean.

The same asymmetry runs in the other direction. I may feel a state directly
without knowing the mechanisms that sustain it. I can experience a visual
field without observing the cortical dynamics that participate in its
construction. I can experience an intention without direct access to all the
preparatory activity, learned valuation, bodily regulation, and environmental
cueing that shaped it.

The first-person condition gives presence without complete mechanism.

The third-person condition gives measurable structure without first-person
presence.

A mature science of consciousness must relate these access conditions without
pretending either one has become the other.

Reports sit at the interface. A report is a public action shaped by a private
occurrence, memory, attention, language, confidence, demand characteristics,
and motor capacity. Reports are therefore neither transparent copies of
experience nor irrelevant noises emitted by a brain. They are transformations.

This matters clinically as well as philosophically. A patient can lack the
motor capacity required for ordinary report while retaining forms of awareness.
Another patient can produce behavior that resembles purposeful response
without supporting the conclusion an observer first draws. The evidence must
be triangulated. No single channel receives automatic supremacy.

DOT will use three methodological commitments:

1. first-person occurrence is part of the phenomenon to be explained;
2. public claims require methods beyond private conviction;
3. disagreement between evidence channels is a research problem, not permission
   to discard whichever channel is inconvenient.

These commitments establish a method. They do not yet establish an ontology.

## The Forbidden Inference

Here is the most important boundary in the opening argument:

> **Bridge boundary — DOT-B1**
>
> Epistemic priority does not entail ontological fundamentality.

Experience may be the condition through which an inquirer consciously knows
anything. It does not follow that experience is the condition through which
anything exists.

The phrase *comes first* is dangerous because it can name two different orders.
Experience comes first in the order of first-person access. That alone does not
prove that consciousness comes first in the order of being, causation, or
cosmic history.

A physicalist can accept that every consciously evaluated argument is
experienced and maintain that experience is identical with, realized by, or
produced by physical processes. A neutral monist can hold that both experience
and physical description arise from a more basic reality. A panpsychist can
assign experiential or proto-experiential properties widely while denying a
single prior consciousness. A cosmopsychist can begin with a cosmic conscious
whole and face the problem of deriving local subjects. An idealist can treat
reality as fundamentally mental while offering a different account of
structure and individuation. An illusionist can explain why cognitive systems
judge themselves to have phenomenal properties without accepting those
properties as ordinarily conceived.

DOT-E0 and epistemic priority do not eliminate these positions.

The boundary must also be enforced in the opposite direction. Precise neural
correlation, successful intervention, and complete functional description do
not automatically establish that consciousness is ontologically identical to
the measured mechanism. Dependence is evidence any theory must explain. The
kind of dependence and the ontology that best explains it remain questions.

Both sides are tempted by overreach.

The consciousness-first theorist can mistake immediacy for fundamentality.

The physicalist can mistake predictive success within physical description for
a completed metaphysics.

Neither move is earned by definition.

## What Present Science Has Earned

Consciousness is no longer absent from empirical research. Competing theories
make claims about recurrent processing, global availability, higher-order
representation, predictive organization, integrated causal structure, and the
neural conditions associated with conscious contents and states. The field has
accumulated substantial results and an equally substantial problem of theory
comparison (Doerig et al. 2021; Seth and Bayne 2022).

The 2025 Cogitate adversarial collaboration gives the Complete Book an
important standard. Proponents of Integrated Information Theory and Global
Neuronal Workspace Theory helped define divergent predictions in advance. The
consortium then examined conscious visual perception in 256 participants using
functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG. Some findings
aligned with predictions from each theory; other findings substantially
challenged key predictions from both. Limited prefrontal representation of
certain conscious dimensions and the absence of a general ignition at stimulus
offset challenged GNWT. The absence of sustained synchronization within
posterior cortex challenged a central tested implication of IIT (Cogitate
Consortium et al. 2025).

The study did not settle what consciousness is.

It demonstrated something equally important for theory building: serious
theories must risk contact with outcomes their authors do not control.

DOT is currently behind that standard. It possesses an architecture, a set of
distinctions, and open questions. It does not yet possess a sufficiently mature
set of preregisterable, discriminating predictions. Calling the architecture
foundational does not close that gap.

The Complete Book exists in part to make the gap impossible to hide.

## DOT's Ontological Commitment

Digital Organism Theory does make a stronger claim than epistemic priority.

It should now be stated without disguise.

> **Ontological hypothesis — DOT-H1**
>
> Consciousness is fundamental rather than reducible to a wholly non-conscious
> base.

The word *fundamental* means that consciousness is not explained away as a late
product whose complete nature can, in principle, be derived without any
conscious primitive or bridge principle. It does not mean that every familiar
feature of human consciousness—language, autobiographical memory, moral
judgment, visual perspective, or personality—is fundamental.

DOT adds a second hypothesis:

> **Architectural hypothesis — DOT-H2**
>
> Fundamental consciousness can differentiate, carry state, and preserve
> consequential continuity.

H2 does not follow automatically from H1. A fundamental reality could be
featureless, changeless, or unable to generate the structure DOT requires.
Differentiation, state, and persistence are additional commitments. They must
be defined and defended rather than placed inside the word *consciousness* and
quietly recovered later.

DOT calls the joint architecture proposed by H1 and H2 **Big C**.

Big C is a model term.

It is not evidence.

At this stage, Big C should not be imagined as a humanlike mind outside the
universe. The model has not earned personality, desire, omniscience, moral
perfection, a plan for each event, or a voice that privately endorses the
author. If later chapters propose purpose, self-preservation, individuation, or
Love at the cosmological level, each property must enter as a new claim with a
new burden.

The theory begins with less.

Consciousness is fundamental.

It can support difference and continuity.

Everything else must be derived.

## Why Entertain the Hypothesis?

An ontological hypothesis is not justified merely because its alternative
contains an explanatory gap. Naming consciousness fundamental can move the
mystery rather than solve it. If Big C is used to explain every unexplained
transition while its own capacities remain undefined, DOT will have replaced
one black box with a larger one.

The hypothesis is worth examining for more limited reasons.

First, it refuses to construct a complete account of reality while leaving
experience outside the primitives of that account. Second, it asks whether the
first-person and third-person structures can be derived as aspects of one
architecture rather than joined only after the physical account is complete.
Third, it places the explanatory gap identified in different forms by Levine
and Chalmers at the beginning of theory construction rather than treating it as
a final inconvenience (Levine 1983; Chalmers 1995).

These are theoretical motivations.

They are not confirmation.

The hypothesis also creates debts that physicalist approaches do not carry in
the same form. DOT must explain why public reality is stable and mathematically
tractable. It must explain why local subjects have bounded access. It must
explain why brain injury, anesthesia, stimulation, development, drugs, sleep,
and disease alter experience so reliably. It must state how causation works
without appealing to a hidden exception whenever physical description becomes
inconvenient. It must distinguish a living interface from a sufficient producer
in a way an experiment could potentially detect.

And it must derive Little c without assuming Little c.

A consciousness-first ontology receives no exemption from the success of
neuroscience.

It inherits that success as a constraint.

## Derivation and Validation

The public edition correctly separated two obligations that must now become the
working rhythm of the complete theory.

**Derivation** asks whether a downstream claim follows from declared premises.

**Validation** asks whether the resulting model survives contact with
observation, measurement, comparison, and consequence.

A derivation can be valid while its premises are false. A model can fit known
observations while receiving no unique support because rival models fit them
equally well. A metaphor can clarify a relation without deriving or validating
anything.

Each level therefore needs a different question:

- Is the term defined?
- Is the inference valid?
- Is the bridge premise defended?
- Is the observation reliable?
- Does the evidence favor this hypothesis over its rivals?
- What result would reduce confidence in the claim?

No observation occurs outside reality, but that does not insulate ontology from
evidence. When an ontology entails a structure, and that structure entails an
observable consequence, measurement can count for or against the chain. Where
multiple ontologies entail the same available observations, the evidence
underdetermines them. The honest label in that case is *metaphysical
underdetermination*, not empirical victory.

The Complete Book will therefore maintain two parallel records: an argument
map showing what depends on what, and a claims ledger showing the status and
burden of each claim.

The reader should never have to guess which ground we are standing on.

## The Research Contract

From this chapter forward, DOT accepts five obligations.

1. **Semantic obligation.** Core terms must remain stable enough that a failed
   claim cannot be rescued by changing what the term meant.
2. **Derivational obligation.** Every major transition must expose the premise
   that carries it.
3. **Comparative obligation.** DOT must confront the strongest version of a
   rival, not the easiest version to dismiss.
4. **Empirical obligation.** Where DOT claims scientific consequence, it must
   specify measurements, predicted differences, and failure conditions.
5. **Revision obligation.** Negative results must be able to remove or weaken a
   claim without being absorbed as another expression of Big C.

The fifth obligation is the hardest.

A theory built to include everything can become a theory threatened by
nothing. It can reinterpret neural dependence as interface, neural independence
as decoupling, order as design, disorder as learning, agreement as recognition,
and disagreement as fear. Such a framework may be psychologically complete. It
is scientifically closed.

DOT cannot become that kind of system.

The model must permit criticism that it cannot reinterpret as pathology.

The evidence must be allowed to say no.

## Where the Book Actually Begins

Two statements are now on the table.

The first is the minimum datum:

> Experience occurs.

The second is DOT's governing ontological hypothesis:

> Consciousness is fundamental.

They are not the same statement.

The first does not prove the second. The second is proposed because DOT asks
whether a consciousness-first architecture can explain more with greater
coherence and fewer unacknowledged bridges than its alternatives. The rest of
the book must pay for that proposal.

This distance between datum and ontology is not a weakness to conceal.

It is the work.
